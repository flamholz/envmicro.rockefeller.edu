---
title: 'Microbial communities: The metabolic rate is the trait'
doi: 10.1016/j.cub.2022.02.002
description: Can the metabolic rates of microbial communities be predicted from their gene content? A comment on a promising approach.
---

Making sense of the metabolism of microbial communities is a daunting yet vital task. Vital because microbes catalyze many critical chemical transformations that recycle elements like carbon, oxygen, sulfur, and nitrogen, supporting the entire biosphere by making them available for reuse. It is vitally important that microbes keep doing us this favor, even as the climate changes and environments change with it (e.g., ocean acidification). Yet there are more than a trillion trillion trillion microbes on our planet (≈10³⁰), representing roughly a million species. A single gram of soil might contain 1,000 species and a trillion individual bugs. How could we possibly understand and predict something so complex?

Avi Flamholz and Dianne Newman comment on a promising approach described in an excellent paper by [Karna Gowda](https://karnagowda.github.io/) and colleagues. Gowda et al. broke ground on an optimistic hypothesis: that communal metabolic rates can be predicted from the gene content of natural environments. If this hypothesis is to hold for soils, it must also hold in the lab. Gowda and colleagues used bacterial denitrification (nitrate "breathing") to learn how to compose predictions of microbial metabolic rates from genome sequences and a small number of lab measurements, achieving surprising quantitative success.
