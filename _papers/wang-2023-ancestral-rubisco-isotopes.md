---
title: Carbon isotope fractionation by an ancestral rubisco suggests that biological proxies for CO₂ through geologic time should be reevaluated
doi: 10.1073/pnas.2300466120
description: A cyanobacterium running on a putative ancestral rubisco fractionates carbon isotopes in ways current models can't explain, complicating how the rock record is read as a proxy for ancient CO₂.
---

The carbon isotope ratios of sedimentary rocks record the history of Earth's carbon cycle, and the dominant model for reading them as a proxy for ancient atmospheric CO₂ is based on modern photosynthetic organisms. How the evolution of photosynthesis might have affected that record has long been an open question.

Led by Renée Wang, we measured both biomass (ε<sub>p</sub>) and enzymatic (ε<sub>Rubisco</sub>) carbon isotope fractionation in a cyanobacterium (*Synechococcus elongatus* PCC 7942) expressing only a putative ancestral Form IB rubisco, more than a billion years old. This strain, "ANC," grows in ambient CO₂ and shows larger ε<sub>p</sub> than wild type despite a much smaller ε<sub>Rubisco</sub>. Surprisingly, ANC's ε<sub>p</sub> exceeded its ε<sub>Rubisco</sub> in every condition tested, contradicting prevailing models. Those models can be rescued by adding fractionation from cyanobacteria's powered inorganic carbon uptake, but doing so makes it harder to estimate past CO₂ from geological data. Fluctuations in the isotope record may therefore reflect the evolving efficiency of carbon fixation as well as changes in atmospheric CO₂.
